1. You're protected, and it's taken seriously

Sexual harassment isn't something you have to just put up with — whoever it's from. A colleague, your manager, a customer, a client on a call, a delivery driver, a contractor. It's unlawful, and Totally Wicked has a legal duty to prevent it.

The duty is getting strongerFrom 30 October 2026, the company must take "all reasonable steps" to prevent it — including from people outside the company, like customers or clients.
Raising it is protectedSince April 2026, a report of sexual harassment can be a legally protected disclosure — you can't lawfully be treated unfairly for speaking up.

You don't need to be an expert in the law to use this module. The point of it is simple: know what counts, know it's not your job to decide whether it's "serious enough," and know exactly what to do next.

2. What counts as sexual harassment

Sexual harassment is unwanted conduct of a sexual nature that violates your dignity, or creates an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment for you — or being treated worse because you rejected or went along with conduct like this.

1
One incident can be enough. It doesn't need to happen more than once, and it doesn't matter whether the other person "meant it that way" — what matters is the effect it had on you.

It can look like:

  • Comments about your body or appearance
  • Sexual jokes, innuendo, or "banter"
  • Unwanted touching, however minor
  • Requests or pressure for sexual favours
  • Being sent sexual images or messages
  • Staring, leering, or standing too close
  • Unwanted messages outside work hours (WhatsApp, social media) connected to your working relationship
  • Comments from a customer, client or other visitor
  • Inappropriate comments on a video call, in a group chat, or over the phone

If you're not sure whether what happened "counts" — that's still worth raising. You don't have to be certain before you say something.

3. What to do in the moment

You're allowed to say soIf you feel safe to, you can tell them plainly that it's unwanted and ask them to stop.
You're allowed to leaveEnd a conversation, hang up a call, step away, or end a transaction — whether it's a colleague, your manager, or a customer or client. You don't have to just get on with it.
You don't have to judge it yourselfIt's not your job to decide in the moment whether it's "serious enough" to matter. That decision isn't yours to make alone.
You don't have to handle it aloneTell your manager or People & Culture as soon as you're able to — see Section 4 for exactly how.
Online
On a call or in a chat, the same rules apply. You can end a video call, leave a group chat, or stop replying — then tell your manager or People & Culture what happened, the same as you would for something that happened in person.

4. How to report it

Normally, tell your managerThey're your first point of contact, and they're expected to take it seriously and log it.
If it's your manager, or you're not comfortableGo straight to People & Culture instead. You never have to raise a concern about someone with that same person.

What happens after you report it:

  • Someone listens, without judging you
  • You're told who else will need to know, and why
  • It's handled as confidentially as possible
  • You're not left to investigate it yourself
  • You're kept updated on what happens next

5. Customers, clients and other third parties

The same protection applies whether it's a colleague or someone outside the company — a customer, a client on a call, a delivery driver, a contractor, a supplier rep. From 30 October 2026, Totally Wicked can be held legally responsible for harassment by any of them, in a store, a warehouse, an office, or on a video call.

You can end the interactionIn person, on a call, or in a chat thread — you're allowed to stop it, whoever it's from.
Your manager should back you upYou shouldn't be expected to just get on with it because "the customer comes first." Report it the same way you would a colleague-to-colleague incident.

A comment doesn't need to become frightening or physical before it counts. Verbal harassment from a customer or client is still harassment, and still worth raising.
